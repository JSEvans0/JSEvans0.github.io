Global Ad Performance

An $11.1M ad budget, and the question of whether it’s pointed at the right platform.

  • SQL

The problem

A company spends $11.1M a year across three ad platforms and gets $54.2M back. That’s a healthy 4.88x blended return, which is exactly the kind of number that stops anyone from looking closer. The question worth asking isn’t whether the portfolio works — it’s whether the money is pointed at the right platform, and what it would cost to find out we’re wrong.

This project exists to demonstrate SQL, so every aggregation lives in SQL rather than pandas. The queries are the artifact: readable files in sql/ with their results and interpretation in the comments, instead of being buried behind a connection string and a DataFrame.

The data

The “Global Ads Performance” dataset from Kaggle — 1,800 daily campaign performance records covering calendar year 2024, across 3 platforms, 4 campaign types, 5 industries and 7 countries. $11.1M spend, $54.2M revenue, 326,812 conversions.

This data is synthetic, and I say so on the first page, not the last. The pipeline is sound — the derived columns reconcile exactly across all 1,800 rows. The distributions are the problem, and they’re the reason the dollar figures here are not a claim about Google, Meta or TikTok.

The model

A star schema in PostgreSQL: five dimensions and one fact table, with a date dimension covering all 366 days.

LayerChoiceWhy
DatabasePostgreSQL 18 Enforces declared types — numeric for money, not float
ModelStar schema, 5 dims + 1 fact Additive measures only; ratios computed at query time
AnalysisSQL throughout Window functions, FILTER, percentile_cont, a bootstrap in pure SQL
OutputStatic HTML + a PDF memo No server, no notebook, nothing to install to read it

Getting the grain right

The fact grain is one row per performance record — not one row per date + platform + campaign type + industry + country. That combination repeats twelve times in this file. A composite key would have failed the load outright, and deduping to force one through would have thrown away real spend. So the fact carries a surrogate key, and 03_fact.sql verifies row counts, orphan rows and measure totals after every load. “The script finished without an error” is not the same claim as “the data arrived intact.”

Why the ratios aren’t stored. Only additive measures live in the fact table — impressions, clicks, spend, conversions, revenue. CTR, CPC, CPA and ROAS are recomputed as a ratio of sums in every aggregate. Storing them invites avg(roas), which weights a $58.00 campaign the same as a $38,453.32 one. On this data that overstates portfolio ROAS by 32% (6.45x against the true 4.88x) — and it inflates the platforms unevenly, distorting the exact comparison the whole recommendation rests on.

What I found

  1. Google absorbs 57.2% of spend and returns 40.7% of revenue — a 16.5-point deficit, at 3.47x blended ROAS against 5.66x for Meta and 7.62x for TikTok.
  2. All 28 of Google’s campaign-type × country cells return below the 4.88x portfolio average. None of TikTok’s 28 do. 61.5% of the book sits in below-average cells.
  3. The gap is entirely a click-cost gap. Conversion rate varies 5% relative across platforms (4.47%–4.71%) and revenue per conversion varies 3% — where Google is actually the highest. CPC varies 112% ($1.02 vs $2.16). You aren’t buying better customers on TikTok; you’re buying the same ones cheaper.
  4. Campaign type, country and industry carry almost no signal. Campaign type spans 4.58x–5.31x against 120% for platform, and TikTok beats Google in all 16 segments by a near-identical 2.20x multiple. That’s one finding restated, not four independent ones.

The recommendation

Shift 20% of Google spend — $1,269,854 — toward TikTok and Meta. Modeled impact is +$5.27M revenue (+9.7%), lifting blended return from 4.88x to 5.35x. Taking it from Google’s six weakest cells (2.62x, rather than trimming evenly at its 3.47x average) is worth a further $1.08M.

The $5.27M is not the number worth quoting. The 54.5% is. The move survives a 54.5% collapse in TikTok’s marginal return before it stops adding revenue. A headline gain is a forecast someone will hold you to; a break-even tolerance tells a director how wrong the analysis can be before the decision changes. That’s the figure that belongs in the memo.

Where it breaks down

I spent about as much effort establishing where this analysis fails as producing the number itself. That section is the reason I’d show anyone this project.

  • The reallocation figure is an upper bound, not a forecast. It assumes TikTok returns its average ROAS on incremental spend. Auction dynamics say it won’t — reach saturates, and a cheap CPC is often the signal of a small audience pool. Nothing in this data can test that, and saying so plainly is the most important sentence in the memo.
  • Country-level conclusions aren’t supported, and I can measure that. A 2,000-iteration bootstrap on TikTok’s country ROAS gives an average 95% interval 3.47 points wide, against a total spread of only 2.22 points between best and worst country. All 21 country pairs overlap. The same test at platform level separates cleanly — TikTok [6.99, 8.30], Meta [5.23, 6.11], Google [3.23, 3.72] — which is exactly why the recommendation is a platform decision and stops there.
  • Cell-level rankings are directional at best. Of 84 platform × campaign type × country cells, the smallest holds 10 records and 0.35% of spend, and 35 hold fewer than 20.
  • The data is synthetic, and not subtly so. 19% of rows return more than 10x and one returns 49x. Revenue per conversion sits in a suspiciously tight $30.21–$299.97 band that looks uniform and independent of every dimension. Conversion rate is effectively constant across platforms, which no real ad account has ever produced. There’s no seasonality across 360 days and no weekday effect (4.89x vs 4.86x).

The modeling, the sizing method and the stress tests are the deliverable. The dollar figures are not.

What I’d do with real data

  • Incrementality testing over observed ROAS. Geo holdouts on both platforms. Nothing here measures whether the revenue would have arrived anyway, which is the question this whole analysis is standing in for.
  • Marginal, not average, ROAS. Fit a response curve per platform from spend step-changes, so the reallocation is priced against the next dollar rather than the average one — the single assumption carrying the most weight here.
  • Check the attribution windows. If Google is credited on a different window than TikTok, part of that 2.2x gap is measurement rather than performance.
  • Creative and audience as dimensions. The star schema has room; “platform” is currently absorbing variance that belongs to creative fatigue and audience overlap.
  • Slowly-changing dimensions. Real accounts rename and restructure campaigns, so dim_campaign would need SCD Type 2 to keep history comparable.
