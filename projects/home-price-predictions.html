Ames Home Price Predictions

What actually determines what a house sells for — and by how much?

  • Python
  • pandas
  • scikit-learn
  • Linear Regression
  • Feature Selection
Code on GitHub

The problem

Given a detailed record of a house — size, quality, condition, garage, porches, lot — can I predict what it will sell for? And more usefully for a seller or an agent: which features are worth money, and roughly how much?

The data

Residential property sales in Ames, Iowa from 2006 to 2010. The raw file arrived with 82 columns across about 2,930 sales: 11 floats, 28 integers, and 43 text columns describing everything from roof material to basement finish quality.

Cleaning the data

This project was mostly a cleaning exercise, and that’s the part I’d point at first.

  1. Dropped the text columns. A linear regression can’t consume raw strings, and the great majority of the missing data lived in those 43 columns. I also dropped Order, which was just a row index. That took 82 columns down to 38.
  2. Handled the remaining nulls deliberately, not uniformly. Garage Yr Blt nulls became 0 — a missing garage year means no garage, which is information, not absence of it. For Lot Frontage, the bulk of values sat between 50 and 85 (mean 69.29) against an overall mean of 69.22, so I filled with 69.25. The remaining 33 null rows I dropped.
  3. Dropped features that were numeric but not meaningful. PID, MS SubClass, Year Built, Year Remod/Add, Garage Yr Blt, Mo Sold and Yr Sold are all numbers a model will happily fit a slope to, and none of them mean what that slope would imply.
  4. Removed outliers. Four rows with Lot Area over 100,000 and five with Misc Val over 5,000.

2,895 rows survived — 98.81% of the original data. Aggressive cleaning on the column side, conservative on the row side.

Exploration

A correlation heatmap against sale price sorted the field quickly:

  • Most positive: Overall Qual, Gr Liv Area (above-grade living area), and Garage Cars.
  • Most negative: Enclosed Porch, Kitchen AbvGr (kitchens above grade), and Overall Cond.

Results

I trained a linear regression model and scored it against a baseline that predicts the mean sale price for every house.

82% of the variation in sale price is explained by the model, and test error is less than half the baseline’s. Train and test scores sit close together, which says the model generalized rather than memorized.

What the coefficients say

Holding everything else constant:

  • Each one-point increase in Overall Quality Score is worth about +$19,436 in sale price — the strongest positive feature in the model.
  • Each additional kitchen above grade is associated with about −$30,655 — the strongest negative feature. Almost certainly a proxy rather than a cause: extra above-grade kitchens tend to signal a subdivided or multi-family property, which sells differently.

Recommendations

  • For a seller: spending on work that raises the overall quality rating is the clearest return available in this data. Cosmetic changes that don’t move that rating don’t show up in price.
  • For an agent pricing a listing: overall quality, above-grade living area, and garage capacity carry most of the signal. That’s a fast, defensible first-pass estimate.
  • Treat the kitchen coefficient carefully. Removing a kitchen will not add $30,000 to a house. It marks a different kind of property, and those should be priced against their own comparables.

What I’d do differently

Dropping all 43 text columns was the right call for a first pass, but it threw away real signal — neighborhood especially. One-hot encoding the categoricals with the most predictive power, and testing a regularized model (Ridge or Lasso) to keep the widened feature set under control, is the obvious next version.
