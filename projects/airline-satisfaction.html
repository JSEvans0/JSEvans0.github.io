Airline Passenger Satisfaction

Which parts of the flying experience actually move the needle on satisfaction?

  • Python
  • pandas
  • scikit-learn
  • Streamlit
  • EDA
Open the live explorer Code on GitHub

The problem

An airline runs a passenger satisfaction survey, so it already knows who is unhappy. What it doesn’t know is which part of the journey to fix first. Cabin comfort, boarding, food, delays and booking are all candidates, and they can’t all be a priority.

My goal: predict passenger satisfaction from the survey responses and flight details, then read the model to rank what actually drives the score.

The data

The airline passenger satisfaction dataset from Kaggle, covering roughly 130,000 survey responses. Alongside the satisfaction score it carries passenger details (age, customer type, class, type of travel), flight facts (distance, departure and arrival delay), and 1–5 ratings across fourteen parts of the service — online boarding, seat comfort, inflight wifi, food and drink, baggage handling, and so on.

Cleaning the data

  1. Nulls. Inspection turned up 310 null values, all concentrated in a single column. With that few rows out of 130,000, dropping them cost nothing.
  2. Outliers. Three columns held values extreme enough to distort a linear fit. I removed 58 rows from Flight Distance, 77 from Departure Delay in Minutes, and 14 from Arrival Delay in Minutes.
  3. Result. A clean, fully numeric frame ready to train on, with well over 99% of the original rows intact.

Method

I fit a linear regression model and compared it against a baseline that simply predicts the mean every time. The baseline matters: without it, a given error number means nothing. I also built an interactive Streamlit app so the visualizations aren’t frozen in a notebook — anyone can slice the data themselves.

Try it yourself: the Airline Satisfaction Explorer lets you browse the dataset, view distributions, and build your own comparisons without touching any code.

The model beats the baseline by a clear margin and explains about 42% of the variance in satisfaction — reasonable for survey data, where a lot of the variation comes down to individual mood. The more valuable output here isn’t the prediction; it’s the ranking of coefficients underneath it.

What drives satisfaction

  • Online boarding had the single largest positive effect on satisfaction.
  • Departure/arrival time convenience had the largest negative effect — inconvenient schedules cost the airline more goodwill than anything else measured.
  • Flight distance, departure delay, arrival delay and age had almost no measurable effect. That’s the counterintuitive one: delays are what passengers complain about, but they aren’t what the survey scores track.

Recommendations

  • Protect the online boarding experience. It’s the strongest positive lever in the data, which makes it the thing most expensive to break.
  • Work on schedule convenience. More convenient departure and arrival times is the clearest available win, and the one with the most room to move.
  • Then look at ease of online booking and food and drink. Both scored weakly enough to be worth attention once the two levers above are handled.
  • Don’t over-invest in delay reduction as a satisfaction play. It may be worth doing for other reasons, but this data doesn’t show it moving satisfaction scores.

What I’d do differently

Satisfaction here is effectively a binary outcome, so a classification model (logistic regression, or a tree-based model) would likely predict better than linear regression and would give me precision and recall to reason about rather than R². I’d also test interaction terms — I suspect delay tolerance depends heavily on travel class and whether the trip is business or personal.
