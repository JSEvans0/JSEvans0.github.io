Higher Education Consulting Analytics

What can a college actually change to graduate more of the students it admits?

  • Python
  • pandas
  • Seaborn
  • EDA
  • Stakeholder Recommendations
Code on GitHub

The problem

A consulting firm advises colleges across the country. Their clients want higher graduation rates, and they want to know where to spend to get them. My job was to find which measurable characteristics of an institution track with graduation — and crucially, which of those a college can actually influence.

The data

Institution-level records covering cost, admissions and staffing. I wrote the data dictionary myself as part of understanding the file:

GroupFields
Institution College name, state, public or private
Admissions Applications received, applications accepted, new students enrolled, % of new students from the top 10% and top 25% of their high school class
Enrollment Full-time undergraduates, part-time undergraduates
Cost In-state tuition, out-of-state tuition, room, board, additional fees, estimated book costs, estimated personal spending
Faculty % of faculty with a PhD, student-to-faculty ratio
Target Graduation rate

Of all of it, the fields I cared about most were the new-student percentages and the faculty measures — those are the ones an institution has real leverage over.

Method

  1. Histograms to locate outliers column by column. Every field except % new stud. from top 25% had them.
  2. Median imputation for nulls, chosen precisely because of those outliers — a mean would have been dragged around by the extreme values the histograms exposed.
  3. A correlation heatmap to see how the fields related to each other and to graduation rate.
  4. Bar charts comparing each candidate factor against graduation rate. These are what the conclusions rest on.

Why median, not mean? Because the histograms came first. Picking the imputation strategy after looking at the distribution — instead of reaching for .fillna(mean) by reflex — is the difference between filling a gap and inventing data.

What I found

  • Faculty headcount tracks with graduation. Lower student-to-faculty ratios line up with higher graduation rates.
  • Faculty credentials track with graduation. A higher share of faculty holding PhDs is associated with better outcomes.
  • Student investment matters. The cost and enrollment patterns point to students who feel more invested in their education being more likely to finish it.

Recommendations

  • Hire more faculty. Of everything measured here, staffing is the lever with the clearest relationship to graduation and the most direct path to action.
  • Hire more credentialed faculty. Raising the share of PhD-holding staff moves the same direction.
  • Increase students’ sense of investment in their education. Students with more at stake in their own program complete it at higher rates.

What I’d do differently

These findings are correlational, and I’d want to say that out loud to a client before they spend money on them — well-funded schools can afford both more faculty and the student support services that likely do some of the actual work. Splitting the analysis by public versus private, and controlling for institution size, would separate “more faculty helps” from “wealthier schools graduate more students.”
